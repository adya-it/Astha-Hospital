Kidney care

Kidney disease and dialysis: what patients and families should know

By the Astha Hospital team · 2026-11-24

The kidneys filter waste and extra water from the blood. When they stop doing this well, waste builds up in the body. Kidney disease often develops silently, which is why regular tests matter, especially if you have diabetes or high blood pressure, the two most common causes.

Possible signs of kidney trouble

  • Swelling of the feet, ankles or around the eyes.
  • Passing much more or much less urine than usual, or foamy or bloody urine.
  • Tiredness, poor appetite, nausea or itching.
  • Breathlessness, or high blood pressure that is hard to control.

A simple blood test for creatinine, a urine test and a blood pressure check can pick up problems early.

What is dialysis?

Dialysis does part of the kidneys' work when they can no longer do enough. In haemodialysis, blood is passed through a machine that cleans it and returns it to the body. It is commonly done around three times a week, with each session lasting several hours, though the schedule is set by your kidney specialist according to your needs. It may be needed for a short time after sudden kidney injury, or long term in advanced kidney disease.

Living with dialysis

  • Keep every session. Missing dialysis can quickly become dangerous.
  • Follow your doctor's advice on fluid, salt, potassium and protein in your diet.
  • Protect the arm with the dialysis access, which is often called a fistula. Do not allow blood pressure checks or injections on that arm and report redness or swelling.
  • Take medicines as prescribed and report breathlessness, chest pain, fever or severe swelling promptly.

Protect your kidneys

  • Keep blood sugar and blood pressure under control.
  • Avoid taking painkillers regularly without medical advice.
  • Drink enough water, stay active and do not smoke.
  • Get kidney tests yearly if you have diabetes, high blood pressure or a family history of kidney disease.
